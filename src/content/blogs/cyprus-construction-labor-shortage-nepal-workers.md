# Cyprus Construction Labor Shortage: How Contractors Can Recruit Skilled Steel Fixers, Carpenters and Site Workers from Nepal

## Executive Summary

Cyprus is building, but it cannot find enough people to build. Contractors, developers and industry bodies have spent much of 2026 warning that the shortage of qualified construction workers is **delaying projects, raising costs and putting pressure on housing delivery**.

Industry representatives have pointed to a gap of roughly 5,000 workers, with the sharpest shortages in skilled trades such as **steel fixers (rebar installers) and formwork carpenters**. At the same time, the departure of many Syrian workers under the government's voluntary repatriation scheme has shrunk the labor pool further.

For contractors, site managers and HR teams, this raises one practical question: **where do you find reliable, trade-qualified workers, and how do you hire them compliantly?**

## The Problem: A Booming Sector Without Enough Hands

Cyprus's construction sector continues to see strong demand across residential, commercial and infrastructure projects. Labor supply has not kept up.

### What the shortage is doing to projects

- **Schedule delays:** Industry bodies such as OSEOK and ETEK have warned that the shortage is slowing completion, particularly on large developments.
- **Rising labor costs:** When crews are scarce, contractors compete for the same workers, pushing up rates and total project costs.
- **Pressure on housing supply:** Delays and cost increases make it harder to deliver new homes at the pace the market needs.
- **Quality and safety risk:** Rushed or under-qualified crews increase rework and site-safety exposure.

### The trades under the most pressure

According to ETEK, the Scientific and Technical Chamber of Cyprus, shortages are acute across almost every category of site worker. The highest demand is for:

- **Steel fixers**
- **Formwork carpenters**
- **Skilled construction workers**
- **Site foremen**
- **Construction supervisors**

### Why the local pool keeps shrinking

- **Syrian repatriation:** Thousands of Syrians living legally in Cyprus have returned home since late 2024, which has thinned the workforce, particularly in construction.
- **Limited EU labor:** Recruiting from within the EU does not cover the volume of skilled trades required.
- **Government outreach to other countries:** Cyprus has been pursuing labor arrangements with countries such as Egypt and India, but employer take-up and paperwork timelines remain open questions.

For employers, waiting on policy to fix the shortage means losing project months. A direct, well-managed recruitment pipeline is a practical alternative.

## How Third-Country Hiring Works in Cyprus

Employers who want to recruit non-EU nationals must follow the procedures set under Cyprus's labor migration policy. In summary:

- The employer applies to the **Department of Labour** at the Ministry of Labour, which assesses whether a genuine shortage exists and may issue initial approval.
- Workers need the appropriate **entry, temporary residence and employment permits** before starting work.
- **Quotas apply.** Industry federations have reported that firms may employ third-country nationals up to 30 percent of their workforce, rising to 50 percent where a collective agreement applies.

**Important:** Rules, quotas and procedures change. Always confirm current requirements with the Cyprus Department of Labour and the relevant migration authorities before submitting applications. Nepali workers are already among the main nationalities of third-country nationals working legally in Cyprus, alongside workers from India, Egypt, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and the Philippines.

Administrative complexity is a real obstacle. A European Commission survey of businesses found that a majority of Cypriot SMEs that tried to hire third-country nationals described the process as difficult, with lengthy procedures cited as a leading problem. A partner who manages documentation and timelines can remove much of that friction.
