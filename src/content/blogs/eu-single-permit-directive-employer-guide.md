Europe's labour shortages are pushing more employers to hire beyond the EU. The revised Single Permit Directive, Directive (EU) 2024/1233, is the main EU-level framework governing how non-EU workers get permission to live and work in a Member State. This guide explains what it changes, where implementation stands, and how HR teams can build a practical compliance process.

*This article is general information, not legal advice. Always confirm requirements with the national immigration authority and qualified counsel in the country where you hire.*

## What Is the Single Permit Directive?

The Single Permit Directive creates one combined application procedure that results in a single permit covering both residence and the right to work in a Member State. The 2024 recast replaced the earlier 2011 Directive (2011/98/EU) with clearer timelines, more predictable procedures, and stronger worker rights.

The Directive does not apply in Denmark or Ireland, which have opted out of EU migration policy in this area.

## Transposition Status: Why Employers Face a Patchwork

Member States had to transpose the Directive into national law by **21 May 2026**. Many missed it. In July 2026 the European Commission sent letters of formal notice, the first step of an infringement procedure, to 17 Member States for failing to communicate complete transposition: Belgium, Bulgaria, Germany, France, Cyprus, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Hungary, Malta, the Netherlands, Austria, Poland, Portugal, Slovenia, Finland and Sweden. Those countries were given two months to respond.

For employers, this means two things. First, the Directive's goals are clear, but national procedures, forms and timelines can still differ widely. Second, a country not named in the notices has not necessarily implemented every provision correctly, so verify locally. Transposition status changes quickly, so check the destination country's current position before filing.

## Key Changes for Employers

**1. One combined application.** The employer or worker files a single application that covers work and residence, instead of running two separate processes.

**2. Apply from abroad or inside the EU.** Applications can be submitted from a third country or, where the worker already holds a valid residence permit, from within the Member State.

**3. Decision deadline.** The Directive sets a maximum of 90 days for authorities to decide on a complete application, which helps with workforce planning.

**4. Changing employer.** Permit holders can change employer during the permit's validity without a completely new permit procedure. Member States may still require notification and can apply a labour-market check in certain cases.

**5. Protection during unemployment.** Workers can keep their status for a limited period without a job, so a redundancy does not lead to immediate loss of residence. Minimum periods are set in the Directive; confirm the national rule.

**6. Limited mobility.** A single permit allows the holder to live and work in the issuing Member State only. It does not give a general right to work in other EU countries.

## Employer Compliance Checklist

- A written job offer or employment contract that meets national labour law and pay standards
- Proof of the worker's qualifications and, for regulated professions such as healthcare, recognition or registration where required
- Valid passport and properly legalised or apostilled civil and academic documents
- Evidence that the employer is lawfully established and in good standing
- A process for notifying authorities about employer changes, role changes or termination
- Internal records of permit validity dates and renewal deadlines

## A 5-Step Compliance Roadmap

**Step 1: Confirm the legal position.** Check whether your destination country has transposed the Directive and which authority handles applications.

**Step 2: Define the role.** Fix the job description, salary, hours and contract length so the application matches the contract.

**Step 3: Verify the candidate.** Check credentials, work history and documents early. Legalisation and translation often take the longest.

**Step 4: File and track.** Submit the single application, record the filing date, and diarise the decision deadline.

**Step 5: Plan onboarding.** Prepare for arrival, residence registration, housing support and post-arrival notification duties.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Assuming one country's procedure applies in another
- Treating the permit as valid across the whole EU
- Starting document legalisation too late
- Ignoring notification duties when an employee changes role or employer
- Relying on outdated guidance written before national laws were adopted

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the Directive apply in every EU country?**
It applies in all Member States except Denmark and Ireland.

**How long should an application take?**
The Directive sets a 90-day limit for a decision, but national implementation and real-world processing times vary.

**Can a worker switch employers?**
Yes, in principle, without a brand-new permit, though notification or a labour-market check may apply under national law.

**Can the worker move to another EU country?**
Not automatically. The permit covers the issuing Member State.
